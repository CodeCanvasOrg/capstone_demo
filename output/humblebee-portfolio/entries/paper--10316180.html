Design of Contextual Filtered Features for Better Smartphone-User Receptivity Prediction

A published study evaluates contextually filtered smartphone and smartwatch features for predicting receptivity to just-in-time health interventions. [PDF p. 1]

Executive Summary

This research asks whether contextual filtering of phone and watch data can improve prediction of a person’s receptivity before a just-in-time intervention. The paper, “Design of Contextual Filtered Features for Better Smartphone-User Receptivity Prediction,” is by Jumabek Alikhanov, Panyu Zhang, Youngtae Noh, and Hakil Kim. It is published in IEEE Internet of Things Journal, volume 11, issue 7, dated 1 April 2024; the source record also lists 2023. This is a research result, not an established HumbleBeeAI product or client case, and owner approval is pending. View publication. [PDF p. 1] [PDF p. 1]

Context and Challenge

Just-in-time digital health support depends on reaching people at a moment when they can receive and engage with an intervention. Timing varies with a person’s state and circumstances, so a predictor must use context rather than rely only on fixed timing. The study addresses receptivity prediction—not whether an intervention improves health or behavior. [PDF p. 1] [PDF p. 1]

Technical Approach

The proposed contextual filtered features (CFFs) combine information from user–smartphone interactions and smartwatch sensor data. The approach mines association rules across subwindow features to identify recurring co-occurrences and construct contextual features. The paper compares this feature-engineering method with conventional time-series statistical features; it does not propose a new classifier. [PDF p. 1] [PDF p. 3] [PDF p. 4]

Implementation

The reported research pipeline collects context, phone interactions and physical-state data alongside experience-sampling questionnaire responses; preprocesses sensor streams; selects features using pairwise-correlation removal and LASSO; then mines association rules and extracts CFFs. The paper describes group 5-fold cross-validation to avoid participant-response overlap and tests logistic regression in the illustrated pipeline, as well as comparing multiple classifiers in its reported average. No deployed application, product integration, or production availability is established by the supplied evidence. [PDF p. 2] [PDF p. 1]

Evaluation

Evaluation used the KEmoPhone dataset: 3,334 experience-sampling responses from 73 participants collected over one week. Across multiple classifiers, average receptivity ROC-AUC was 56.5% with CFFs versus 54.7% with conventional features—an absolute difference of 1.8 percentage points. The conventional features are statistical time-series measures over an 80-minute window. The source describes participant-grouped 5-fold cross-validation, but does not provide enough detail here to reconstruct all test conditions or uncertainty estimates; the reported figures are not independently validated. [PDF p. 1] [PDF p. 2]

Results and Business Impact

The measured result is a modest improvement in model discrimination on the study dataset. It is not evidence of improved intervention adherence, clinical outcomes, or business impact: those outcomes were outside the study’s scope, and no client or product results are supplied. [PDF p. 1] [PDF p. 4]

Limitations and Next Steps

The evidence is limited to receptivity prediction using the specified dataset and evaluation. Clinical or behavioral effectiveness, performance in other populations or deployments, and operational integration have not been established in the supplied material. The paper states that research code would be released in Jupyter Notebooks; current availability is not confirmed here. No future product plans are provided. [PDF p. 1]

Conclusion

This published research tests whether context-aware feature engineering can improve predictions of receptivity to timely health support. Its reported advantage over conventional features is specific to the KEmoPhone evaluation and should not be interpreted as proof of clinical benefit or a production-ready capability. [PDF p. 1]
